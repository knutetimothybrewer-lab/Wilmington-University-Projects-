Question 0 · What is AI?

Four kinds of AI — each one sits inside the last

Four kinds (1.5 min). Click each ring or tab in order. Rule-based: people write the rules. Machine learning: the machine finds the rules from examples. Deep learning: machine learning with many-layered neural networks. Generative: deep learning that makes new text, images, audio. Today's chatbots live in the innermost circle. Reassure: "Every ring is still AI — newer isn't automatically better, just different."

1 · Machine Learning

Five steps: how a machine learns from examples

Five steps (1.5 min). Use the flash-card analogy: collect examples → label them → find patterns → quiz on NEW examples → improve and repeat. The key sentence: "Nobody typed the rules. The machine inferred them from examples." Point out step 4: the fox gets mislabeled — models only know what their examples taught them.

1 · Machine Learning Hands-on

A tiny neural network: signals, layers, knobs

Tap the clues on the left to switch them on. Signals flow right; cyan = “pushes toward”, pink = “pushes against”.

Neural net (1 min). Inputs are clues, the middle layer combines clues, outputs are guesses. Each line has a "knob" (weight). Scramble: the same clues give nonsense. Train: the knobs are nudged until answers match the examples. Real models have the same shape with vastly more knobs — that's all "learning" means.
1 · Machine Learning Hands-on

Two jobs: pick a label or create new text

🏷️ Classifier picks from a menu

✍️ Generator writes something new

Same message in. A classifier answers from a fixed menu; a generator composes new words — and may write something different each time.

Label vs create (1 min). Classic machine learning mostly picks labels (spam/not spam, grade bands). Generative AI produces new content, one token at a time — and therefore can differ on every run. Press "Generate again" and point out the wording change. This sets up chapter 2.

2 · LLMs & Tokens

A large language model is a very good next-word predictor

LLM = a neural network trained on a huge amount of text to predict what comes next. Large = enormous numbers of adjustable knobs (called parameters) and a huge training library. Think: autocomplete, but trained on a library and able to keep going.
LLM definition + five steps (1.5 min). Read the definition once. Then step through: prompt → tokens → numbers → score every possible next token → pick one, add it, repeat. Stress "predict" not "look up": it is not searching a database of facts.

What to notice

  • Common short words = one token.
  • Rare or long words get chopped into pieces.
  • Spaces and punctuation count too.
  • The model never sees letters — only token numbers.

Simplified splitter for teaching. Real tokenizers cut text differently.

Tokens (1 min). Type a long word and show it breaking up. Why it matters: models count and limit text in tokens, not words; it also explains odd failures such as miscounting the letters in a word. Tick "show token numbers" so the room sees that the model works with numbers.
2 · LLMs & Tokens Hands-on

Choosing the next word, one step at a time

The model scores every possible next token. Click one to choose it — or let the model roll the dice.

Next word (1 min). Click "door", then "and"… Build a sentence together with the room. Key insight: at every step there is a list of plausible options with probabilities; one is chosen and the process repeats. That's all a chatbot reply is — hundreds of these picks in a row.

How much text can it see at once? (the “context window”)

Window size:

Models only “see” what fits inside the window. Old messages that fall outside are not remembered — so restate key facts in long chats.

Context window (1 min). Start small: the model has "forgotten" the student's name and can't help. Drag the window wider: the answer improves. Real windows are much larger than this toy, but they are finite. Practical tip for teachers: in a long chat, paste key details again.

3 · Data Centers

Chatbots live in giant buildings full of computers

Where they live (1 min). This is a new kind of building to most people. Step through the four systems: servers with special chips do the thinking; power feeds them; cooling removes their heat; the network connects them to you. "The cloud" is just somebody else's building."

    3 · Data Centers · Questions for your school

    Three school questions hiding in that building

    🔒

    Privacy

    Whatever you type travels to those machines. Where does it go, and who can see it?

    • Is student information ever typed in? Should it be?
    • Is what we type stored, and for how long?
    • Is it used to train future models?
    • Does the vendor meet our student-data laws and policies?
    ⚡

    Electricity

    Training and answering both draw real power. Every message uses some.

    • Which tasks genuinely need AI and which don't?
    • Where does the building get its power?
    • Does the provider publish its energy use?
    • How do we teach students this hidden cost?
    💧

    Cooling

    Computers turn electricity into heat. Removing it takes air, water, or both.

    • What cooling method does the provider use?
    • Is the building in a place short on water?
    • Is the heat reused or just released?
    • Does the provider report its water use?

    ☑ Tap a question to tick it off as your school discusses it. We're raising questions, not quoting numbers — answers differ by provider and place.

    School questions (1.5 min). These are questions, not verdicts — no numbers quoted on purpose; they differ by provider and location. Ask the room: "Which of these would your principal ask first?" Tick a few. These make a good agenda item for a staff meeting or tech committee.

    4 · Hallucinations

    Why chatbots make things up

    The study above was invented for this lesson. It does not exist.

    1 · Plausible ≠ trueIt predicts likely-sounding words. It doesn't look facts up.
    2 · Gaps & errorsIts training text had holes and mistakes — it can't tell which.
    3 · Built to be helpfulIt would rather answer than admit it doesn't know.
    4 · Confident toneFluent writing sounds right even when it's wrong.

    Hallucination = a fluent, confident answer that is false or invented.

    Why it happens (1 min). Let the chat play. The made-up study sounds perfect: authors, journal, finding. Then the stamp. Four reasons — all follow from "it predicts plausible words". Reassure: newer tools hallucinate less, especially when connected to search, but none are immune.

    4 · Hallucinations

    How to check in five moves

    🔎 Watch a check happen

    1️⃣Search the exact title in a library or Google Scholar.
    2️⃣Does the journal exist? Look it up.
    3️⃣Open the link or DOI. Does it land on that paper?
    4️⃣Read the source. Does it really say what the bot claims?
    5️⃣Ask a second source — a colleague, librarian, or another reliable site.

    🚩 Red flags: perfect-sounding statistics · no link · authors you can't find · “every student, every subject”.

    How to check (1 min). Watch the two searches: the real title finds a result, the invented one finds nothing. Five moves; the fourth is the one people skip — a real paper may still not say what the chatbot claims. Your school librarian is your best ally here.
    4 · Hallucinations

    Five habits for using AI wisely

    1✅Check it

    Treat every claim, quote and citation as a draft until you verify it.

    In class: “Find the source” warm-up.
    2🎯Be specific

    Give grade level, goal and format. Clear prompts get better drafts.

    In class: rewrite a vague prompt.
    3🔒Protect privacy

    No student names, grades, or health details in a chatbot.

    In class: spot the private info.
    4🧑‍🏫You're the teacher

    AI drafts; you decide. Your judgement and relationships can't be outsourced.

    In class: edit the AI's draft.
    5🗣️Be open

    Say when and how AI was used — and model that for students.

    In class: add an “AI used” note.
