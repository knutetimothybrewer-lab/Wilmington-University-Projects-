AI Literacy for Educators

The driving question

How can educators develop AI literacy to make informed, ethical, and effective decisions regarding artificial intelligence in teaching, learning, leadership, and educational practice?

Scroll to begin

02The promise

From curious to confident to leading.

AI Literacy for Educators is a hands-on, fully online course for general and special education teachers, coaches, and school leaders. It takes you from AI basics to a classroom-ready, policy-compliant AI workflow you can defend. No prior AI experience needed.

03The three superpowers

Three skills run through every week.

1

Spot hallucinations

AI can sound confident and be wrong. You will learn to verify facts, sources, and answers, and to resist automation bias.

Try it: tap the phrases you doubt, then press Verify.

Interactive demo. The paragraph below contains one false claim, planted for practice. Press the phrases you doubt, then press the Verify button to learn which claim was false.

A staged example, written for this demo

The water cycle moves water around our planet. Heat from the sun makes water evaporate from oceans and lakes. Scientists estimate that about 97% of Earth's water is fresh water, mostly in rivers and lakes. As vapor rises and cools, it condenses into clouds. When droplets grow heavy, they fall as precipitation and collect in rivers, lakes, and underground.

The false claim in this passage is the one about Earth's water: about 97% is salt water, mostly in the oceans. Only about 3% is fresh, and most of that is frozen in ice caps and glaciers. The AI sounded just as confident about it as about the true statements.

2

Write good inputs

The same AI gives wildly different results depending on what you give it. The five-part prompt framework: Role, Task, Context, Format, Constraints.

Try it: add one part at a time and watch the simulated response improve.

Interactive demo. A one-line prompt is rebuilt, part by part, into the five-part framework. Use the Add the next part button, and read how the simulated response improves after each one.

Your prompt

“Make a worksheet about fractions.”

  1. RoleYou are a fourth-grade math teacher.
  2. TaskWrite a six-question practice set on comparing fractions with unlike denominators.
  3. ContextMy class just learned equivalent fractions. A few students read below grade level and several are English learners.
  4. FormatPut it in a one-page table with columns for question, visual hint, and answer. Add an answer key at the bottom.
  5. ConstraintsKeep each question under 12 words. Use no student names or personal data. Tell me anything you are unsure of.

3

Turn drafts into good outputs
