Question 2 · How do I ask so it gets it right?

Five parts: C · R · A · F · T

The five parts (2 min). Walk the list. Context: what the AI cannot see about your class. Role: who it should act as, which steers vocabulary and what it notices. Action: one clear verb, a number, a purpose. Format: the shape of the output before you get it. Target and tone: the audience, reading level, voice, and limits. Click each step and compare the weak line with the strong one. Order does not matter; completeness does.

After the first answer

The first answer is a draft. Steer it.

Rule of thumb: change one thing per follow-up so you can see what worked.

Follow-ups (1.5 min). Click the moves in any order. “Ask me three questions first” is the favourite: the AI interviews you instead of guessing. “What did you assume?” exposes hidden defaults. Remind the room that the conversation is the tool: they do not have to get it perfect in one prompt, they have to get it steerable.
Take this home

Your CRAFT template, and one safety check.

Before you press Enter

  • No student names, IDs, photos or grades.
  • No health, IEP or 504 details. Describe the need instead: “a student reading two grades below level.”
  • If you would not put it on a hallway bulletin board, do not paste it.
  • Use the tools your school or district has approved. Student records are protected by laws such as FERPA in the US.
Template + privacy (1 min). The copy button puts a plain-text version on their clipboard. Fill-ins are in square brackets. Privacy is part of prompt design: you can get an excellent answer without any student identifying data. Invite them to share their district’s policy in the discussion board.

Your turn

Offload it, co-pilot it, or keep it?

Sort (2 min). Eight tasks, one at a time. Take a vote, then click. Offload: routine, easy to check. Co-pilot: you and the AI trade ideas, you decide. Keep: it depends on your knowledge of real students, or it is verification itself. The “check the answer key” card surprises people: verification is the one task you cannot hand to the thing that might be wrong. At the end, the summary shows time freed by sorting well.
Question 5 · How do I know it is any good?

Look through five lenses

Five lenses (1.5 min). Accuracy: is it right? Credibility: who stands behind it, do the sources exist? Bias: whose view is missing? Reliability: would it say this again, and does a trusted source agree? Educational value: does it teach what you meant it to? Each has a quick test and a red flag. A single output often fails one lens and passes the rest, which is why we use all five.
Your turn

An AI wrote this handout. Click every sentence.

Why did colonists protest the Tea Act?

My goal: students explain why people protested (cause and effect).
In December 1773, colonists dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor to protest the Tea Act. The protest was led by Benjamin Franklin, who climbed aboard the ships himself. A 2019 Harvard study found that 9 in 10 colonists supported the protest. Every colonist agreed that the tea had to go. Quick check: What year did the Boston Tea Party happen? (Answer: 1773)
Inspect (3 min). Let the room call out which sentence is wrong before you reveal. Sentence 1 is correct: 342 chests is the standard figure, so not everything is flagged. Sentence 2: Franklin was in London. Sentence 3: no such study; you will check this laterally next. Sentence 4: one group stands in for everyone. Sentence 5: recall only, but your goal was cause and effect. Then press “Run the same prompt again”: the “fact” changes. That is Reliability.

A fictional site, for illustration. Stanford researchers (2017) found professional fact-checkers left unfamiliar sites quickly to check them elsewhere, while many historians and students stayed and judged by appearance.
Lateral reading (2 min). Left: the habit most of us have. The page looks professional, has an About page, says it is trusted. We read down and our confidence climbs. Right: the fact-checker’s habit. Within seconds she opens new tabs: who runs this, what do others say about it, does anyone else report the claim? Confidence now comes from evidence. For AI: an AI answer is not a source, it is a lead. Take the claim and read it laterally.
Practice · SIFT

Check one claim laterally.

The AI said: “A 2019 Harvard study found that 9 in 10 colonists supported the protest.”
SIFT (2 min). SIFT is a four-move routine from Mike Caulfield: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to the original. The searches shown are simulated for the demo; in class, do it live with a real claim and a real browser. The outcome here: no such study exists, historians describe divided opinion. The AI produced a plausible-sounding source. Discard the claim, keep the question.
